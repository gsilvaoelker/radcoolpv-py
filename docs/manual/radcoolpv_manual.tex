\section{Purpose and scientific status}

\code{radcoolpv} calculates optical properties of periodic photonic structures
for radiative cooling and couples them to a silicon PV thermal/electrical model.
The active implementation is based on the MATLAB+Lua/S4
\code{matlab-radCoolPV} model. Live optics use only the Stanford S4 Python
extension. S4 is imported lazily; reduced-spectrum and free-form readers can run
without it.

The package reports spectral reflectance \(R\), transmittance \(T\), total
absorptance \(A\), silicon-layer absorptance \(A_{\mathrm{Si}}\), and, under the
conditions stated in Section~\ref{sec:kirchhoff}, emittance. The thermal stage
reports operating temperature, I--V characteristics, maximum-power-point output,
efficiency, and a temperature reduction relative to a user-specified reference.

The library is not a universal PV module model. Its central assumptions are a
periodic coherent optical unit cell, scalar material permittivities, a lumped
steady-state thermal balance, and the silicon diode model inherited from the
MATLAB code. Validation status is evidence-labelled in
Section~\ref{sec:validation}; a passing software test is not treated as proof of
physical validity.

\section{Optical model}

\subsection{The S4 problem}

The YAML structure is ordered from the incident medium toward one
semi-infinite terminal layer. S4 expands the in-plane fields in a Fourier basis
of size \code{simulation.s4\_modes}. Flat, circular, rectangular, and
polygonal regions are supplied to S4 analytically; spheres and hemispheres use
the configured stack of circular slices.

At each wavelength, direction, and polarization, the code evaluates the
integrated normal Poynting flux. With incident flux \(P_{\mathrm{inc}}\),

\begin{align}
R &= -\frac{P_{\mathrm{top}}^-}{P_{\mathrm{inc}}},\\
T &= \frac{P_{\mathrm{bottom}}^+}{P_{\mathrm{inc}}},\\
A &= 1-R-T.
\end{align}

Unlike the old Lua output, \(T\) is normalized by incident flux at oblique
incidence. The silicon-layer absorptance uses the net flux at the two interfaces
of the silicon layer itself:

\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{Si}} =
\frac{P_{\mathrm{net}}(z_{\mathrm{Si,top}})
      -P_{\mathrm{net}}(z_{\mathrm{Si,bottom}})}
     {P_{\mathrm{inc}}}.
\end{equation}

This prevents absorption in a layer below silicon from being assigned to the
PV cell.

Because \(A\) is formed as \(1-R-T\), energy conservation is structural rather
than a diagnostic: what it tests is the flux normalisation, not the solver. The
residual \(|R+T+A-1|\) computed in memory is at machine precision
(\(\sim10^{-16}\)); the \(\sim10^{-7}\) seen in stored spectra is the
\code{\%.6e} text format, not a physical error.

\subsection{Unit cell and shape discretisation}
\label{sec:geometry}

\code{geometry.lattice.type} selects the Bravais cell. \code{square} builds
vectors \((x,0),(0,x)\) and \emph{ignores} \code{lattice.y}. \code{hexagonal}
builds \((x,0),(0,y)\) and additionally places a second copy of every circular
motif at the cell centre \((x/2,y/2)\): the hexagonal array is represented as a
centred-rectangular supercell with two motifs. For nearest-neighbour pitch
\(p\), the correct entries are

\begin{equation}
x=\sqrt{3}\,p,\qquad y=p,
\end{equation}

which places all nearest neighbours at distance \(p\). The centred motif is
added only for \code{cylinder}, \code{sphere}, and \code{semisphere}; for
\code{triangle} and \code{grating} a hexagonal setting changes the cell vectors
but yields a single motif, so those shapes are effectively rectangular.

Curved shapes are stacks of uniform slices, since S4 layers are
\(z\)-invariant. For a sphere or hemisphere of radius \(a\) discretised into
\(N=\)~\code{discretization\_layers} slices of thickness
\(\delta=2a/N\), slice \(i\) is a circle of radius

\begin{equation}
r_i=\sqrt{\max\!\left(a^2-(y_i-a)^2,\;0\right)},
\qquad y_i = 2a-\tfrac{\delta}{2}-i\,\delta .
\end{equation}

A hemisphere keeps the top \(\lfloor N/2\rfloor\) whole slices and, for odd
\(N\), one half-thickness slice straddling the equator, so the dome spans
exactly \(a\) for either parity. A triangle of base \(b\) and height \(H\) uses
rectangles of half-width \(b\,y/2H\). \code{grating} is a one-dimensional
lamellar layer: ridges of \code{photonic\_material} with period
\code{lattice.x}, duty \(r\), and a vacuum groove of half-width
\((1-r)\,p/2\) spanning the full cell in \(y\).

\(N\) is a convergence parameter, not a free choice: a slice stack is a
staircase approximation of a curved surface, and only \code{cylinder},
\code{grating}, and \code{flat} are represented exactly.

\subsection{Directions and polarization}

One YAML case supports one of three modes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{normal}: \(\theta=0\);
  \item \code{specific}: one user-defined polar angle \(\theta\) and azimuth
        \(\phi\);
  \item \code{hemispherical}: a two-dimensional \((\theta,\phi)\) quadrature.
\end{itemize}

\code{polarization} is \code{TE}, \code{TM}, or \code{unpolarized}. TE and TM
are never assumed equal solely because \(\theta=0\); anisotropic unit cells such
as one-dimensional gratings can give different normal-incidence responses.
Unpolarized output is

\begin{equation}
X_{\mathrm{unpol}}=\frac{X_{\mathrm{TE}}+X_{\mathrm{TM}}}{2},
\qquad X\in\{R,T,A,A_{\mathrm{Si}}\}.
\end{equation}

For one polarization \(p\), the hemispherical property is

\begin{equation}
X_{\mathrm{h},p}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}
\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}
X_p(\lambda,\theta,\phi)\cos\theta\sin\theta
\,d\theta\,d\phi.
\end{equation}

The implementation applies Gauss--Legendre quadrature in
\(u=\sin^2\theta\) and a uniform periodic azimuth rule. It avoids the grazing
endpoint and includes a separate zero-weight normal probe for the PV
luminescence term. Quadrature weights sum to one. A true hemispherical result
therefore requires convergence with respect to both
\code{hemisphere\_theta\_points} and
\code{hemisphere\_azimuth\_points}.

\subsection{Emittance and Kirchhoff's law}
\label{sec:kirchhoff}

The code labels total absorptance as emittance using directional Kirchhoff
reciprocity,

\begin{equation}
\epsilon_p(\lambda,\theta,\phi,T)
=A_p(\lambda,\theta,\phi,T).
\end{equation}

This identification requires a passive reciprocal structure in local thermal
equilibrium, evaluated with the same wavelength, direction, polarization, and
temperature-dependent material state. It is not automatically valid for
nonreciprocal, active, nonlinear, or nonequilibrium emitters. The present
material models are temperature-independent.

\subsection{Materials and numerical validity}

Tabulated models interpolate \(n\) and \(k\) linearly and use
\(\epsilon=(n+ik)^2\). Requests outside a table's wavelength interval raise an
error; endpoint clamping and silent extrapolation are forbidden. Validation E
uses the unmodified refractiveindex.info Olmon evaporated-Au record. Its
SiO2 and Si refractive-index tables are inherited from
\code{matlab-radCoolPV}: the former is labelled Palik/Kitamura; the latter has
no bibliographic source and is made lossless to implement the paper's explicit
nonabsorbing-Si assumption. The current refractiveindex.info database has no
records labelled with the paper-cited Palik/Kitamura Si or SiO2 sources. This
provenance gap is retained as a limitation rather than silently replacing the
models.

Every scientific result must demonstrate:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item passivity and finite \(R,T,A,A_{\mathrm{Si}}\), with
        \(R+T+A\approx1\);
  \item convergence in S4 Fourier basis size;
  \item for hemispherical results, convergence in both angular counts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Atmosphere, thermal balance, and PV model}

\subsection{Atmospheric and thermal radiation}

For zenith transmittance \(\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)\), the directional
atmospheric emittance is

\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda,\theta)
=1-\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)^{1/\cos\theta}.
\end{equation}

The surface--atmosphere product is integrated with the same angular quadrature
as the surface emittance. With Planck spectral radiance \(B_\lambda(T)\),

\begin{align}
P_{\mathrm{rad}}(T)
 &= \pi\int \epsilon_{\mathrm{h}}(\lambda)B_\lambda(T)\,d\lambda,\\
P_{\mathrm{atm}}(T_{\mathrm{amb}})
 &= \pi\int
 \left\langle\epsilon\epsilon_{\mathrm{atm}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{h}}
 B_\lambda(T_{\mathrm{amb}})\,d\lambda,\\
P_{\mathrm{conv}}(T)
 &= h\left(T-T_{\mathrm{amb}}\right).
\end{align}

The convection coefficient \(h\) is the total effective nonradiative
coefficient for the reference area used by the balance. It is an external
lumped-model input and is not deduced from geometry or weather data. A
per-surface coefficient must be combined explicitly by the user; the library
does not multiply \(h\) by an assumed number of exposed surfaces.

\code{rad\_power} returns the radiance integral
\(\int\epsilon(\lambda)B_\lambda(T)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\) in
\(\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}\), with

\begin{equation}
B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}
\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right)-1\right]^{-1},
\end{equation}

evaluated on the micrometre grid. The caller multiplies by \(\pi\) to integrate
the cosine-weighted hemisphere, so \(\pi\int B_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\) over
a broad band reproduces \(\sigma T^4\); that identity is a useful check on any
new wavelength range. Because the integral is trapezoidal on the configured
grid, a range that truncates the Planck peak silently understates
\(P_{\mathrm{rad}}\): at \(300~\mathrm{K}\) the peak lies near
\(9.7~\mathrm{\mu m}\) and a \(4\)--\(30~\mathrm{\mu m}\) window is the usual
minimum.

\subsection{Solar absorption and steady state}

Total absorbed solar power is

\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{sun}}=\int A(\lambda)I_{\mathrm{AM1.5G}}(\lambda)\,d\lambda.
\end{equation}

\begin{sloppypar}
For a literature cooling curve that prescribes absorbed solar power directly,
the \code{absorbed\_solar\_power} key in the \code{thermal} section overrides
this integral. Validation E uses the paper value
\(808~\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\).
\end{sloppypar}

The cooling-power convention is

\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{cool}}(T)=
P_{\mathrm{rad}}-P_{\mathrm{atm}}+P_{\mathrm{conv}}-P_{\mathrm{sun}}
+P_{\mathrm{MPP}}+P_{\mathrm{nt}},
\end{equation}

where \(P_{\mathrm{MPP}}\) is electrical power removed from the cell and
\(P_{\mathrm{nt}}\) is the non-thermal luminescence term. Equilibrium satisfies
\(P_{\mathrm{cool}}(T_{\mathrm{eq}})=0\).

Cooling-curve cases specify their temperature interval in YAML. Standard PV
cases evaluate \(T_{\mathrm{amb}}\) through \(T_{\mathrm{amb}}+150~\mathrm{K}\).
If the balance does not bracket a root, the run raises an error. It never
reports a sweep endpoint as an equilibrium temperature.

\subsection{I--V calculation}

The short-circuit current uses IQE, silicon-layer absorptance, and the solar
photon flux up to the silicon bandgap:

\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{sc}}=q\int_0^{\lambda_g}
\mathrm{IQE}(\lambda)A_{\mathrm{Si}}(\lambda)
\Phi_{\mathrm{sun}}(\lambda)\,d\lambda.
\end{equation}

At each \(T,V\), the current density \(J\) solves

\begin{multline}
0=-J+\frac{V-JR_s}{R_{\mathrm{sh}}}
+J_0(T)\left[
\exp\left(\frac{q(V-JR_s)}{k_BT}\right)-1\right]\\
+J_{\mathrm{Auger}}(T,V)-J_{\mathrm{sc}}.
\end{multline}

Output power is \(-JV\). Each voltage is solved with \code{scipy.optimize.fsolve},
warm-started from the previous voltage's solution, which keeps the strongly
exponential diode equation in its convergence basin across the sweep.

The saturation current is itself a spectral integral rather than a fitted
constant, using the same IQE and silicon absorptance against a blackbody photon
flux at the cell temperature:

\begin{equation}
J_0(T)=q\int_0^{\lambda_g}
\mathrm{IQE}(\lambda)A_{\mathrm{Si}}(\lambda)\Phi_{\mathrm{bb}}(\lambda,T)
\,\mathrm{d}\lambda .
\end{equation}

Auger recombination uses the intrinsic carrier concentration

\begin{equation}
n_i = 5.29\times10^{19}
\left(\frac{T}{300}\right)^{2.54}
\exp\!\left(\frac{-6726}{T}\right)~\mathrm{cm^{-3}},
\end{equation}

with a coefficient \(A(T)\) interpolated from five tabulated points
(\(195\)--\(372~\mathrm{K}\), \(3.03\)--\(4.55\times10^{-31}~\mathrm{cm^6/s}\)),
giving

\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{Auger}}=2qA(T)n_i^3 d_{\mathrm{Si}}
\exp\!\left(\frac{3qV}{2k_BT}\right).
\end{equation}

Extrapolating outside that temperature range flattens to the endpoint values,
so results far outside \(195\)--\(372~\mathrm{K}\) should be treated as
unsupported.

The band-gap wavelength preserves the MATLAB scalar-reduction convention: the
original evaluated \(E_{g0}-\alpha T^2/(T+\beta)\) with matrix right division on
a row vector, which collapses to the single scalar
\(\alpha\,(T^2\!\cdot\!(T+\beta))/((T+\beta)\!\cdot\!(T+\beta))\). One
\(\lambda_g\) therefore serves the whole temperature sweep. This is a
model-compatibility choice, not a temperature-resolved band-gap model, and it
sets the upper limit of every spectral integral above.

\subsection{Solving for the operating point}
\label{sec:fixedpoint}

\(P_{\mathrm{MPP}}\) and the luminescence term depend on \(V_{\mathrm{mpp}}\),
which depends on the temperature that the balance is trying to find. The code
resolves this by fixed-point iteration from \(V_{\mathrm{mpp}}=0.65~\mathrm{V}\):
assemble the balance, locate \(T_{\mathrm{eq}}\), re-evaluate the power curve
there, and refine \(V_{\mathrm{mpp}}\), stopping once successive values agree to
the configured tolerance. Failure to settle raises a
\code{RuntimeWarning} naming the last value rather than silently returning it.

Three numerical choices matter for reproducibility, and all three depart
deliberately from the MATLAB original:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Equilibrium temperature} is the linearly interpolated zero
        crossing of \(P_{\mathrm{cool}}\), not the nearest grid point. A balance
        that never crosses zero, or is already non-negative at the lowest swept
        temperature, raises an error naming the swept interval. An endpoint is
        never reported as a solution.
  \item \textbf{MPP and \(V_{\mathrm{mpp}}\)} come from a three-point parabolic
        fit through the largest sample and its neighbours, so they are not
        quantised to the \code{thermal.voltage} step. It falls back to the raw
        sample at a boundary or for collinear points.
  \item \textbf{\(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\)} is the interpolated crossing of \(-J\). The
        MATLAB \code{find} returned the last grid point before the crossing,
        biasing \(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\) low by up to one step
        (\(7~\mathrm{mV}\) on the default sweep).
\end{itemize}

Every equilibrium quantity is then reported at that same interpolated
temperature, so \(T_{\mathrm{eq}}\), \(P_{\mathrm{MPP}}\),
\(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\), and the fill factor describe one consistent operating
point.

\subsection{Temperature reduction}

When \code{thermal.reference\_temperature} is supplied,

\begin{equation}
\Delta T_{\mathrm{reduction}}
=T_{\mathrm{reference}}-T_{\mathrm{eq}}.
\end{equation}

The user must define the reference physically, for example the operating
temperature of a bare module under the same boundary conditions. The library
does not invent or silently substitute a reference.

\section{Numerical methods and their failure modes}
\label{sec:numerics}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{34mm}p{40mm}p{52mm}}
\toprule
Step & Method & Fails when\\
\midrule
Polar quadrature & Gauss--Legendre in \(u=\sin^2\theta\) &
too few nodes for a sharply directional emitter\\
Azimuth quadrature & uniform periodic rule &
anisotropic cells with few azimuth points\\
Material \(n,k\) & linear interpolation, extrapolation refused &
requested \(\lambda\) outside the table (raises)\\
Spectral integrals & trapezoidal on the configured grid &
grid truncates the Planck peak or undersamples the solar band\\
Band edges & first sample within a MATLAB \code{find} tolerance &
coarse grids miss 1.12, 4, 8, 13 \(\mu\)m and silently return 0\\
Diode I--V & \code{fsolve}, warm-started per voltage &
no bracket, or a jump too large between voltages\\
MPP, \(V_{\mathrm{mpp}}\) & three-point parabolic refinement &
peak on a sweep boundary (falls back to the sample)\\
\(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\) & interpolated \(-J\) zero crossing &
sweep does not bracket it (raises)\\
\(T_{\mathrm{eq}}\) & interpolated \(P_{\mathrm{cool}}\) zero crossing &
no crossing in range (raises, never returns an endpoint)\\
\(V_{\mathrm{mpp}}\) coupling & fixed-point iteration &
does not settle (warns, naming the last value)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{sloppypar}
The band-edge row deserves emphasis because it fails quietly rather than
loudly. Both \code{optical\_band\_averages} and \code{pv\_band\_averages}
reproduce the MATLAB convention of taking the first sample within a tolerance
of each edge; if the wavelength grid has no sample near an edge, that band
average returns \(0.0\) instead of raising. The default
\(0.3\)--\(30~\mathrm{\mu m}\) grid with \(n=2000\) satisfies every edge. A
coarse grid may not, and a zero in a band-average column should always be read
as a possible missing edge before it is read as physics.

The general-purpose \code{band\_average} avoids the tolerance trap by selecting
on value, but it is not immune to the wider problem: a band with no samples in
range returns \(0.0\), and a band only partly covered by the grid is averaged
over the covered part alone and so reads low. Neither raises. Any band average
must therefore be taken together with the wavelength range that produced it.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{YAML examples}

\subsection{One file with multiple cases}

A top-level \code{cases} list lets one YAML file define several named runs.
The CLI executes them in order:

\begin{lstlisting}
cases:
  - name: normal_TE
    run: {optics: true, thermal: false}
    simulation:
      wavelength: {min: 8.0, max: 13.0, n: 201}
      angles: normal
      polarization: TE
    # geometry, structure, materials, and data follow
  - name: directional_TM
    run: {optics: true, thermal: false}
    simulation:
      wavelength: {min: 8.0, max: 13.0, n: 201}
      angles: specific
      polar_angle_deg: 35.0
      azimuth_angle_deg: 90.0
      polarization: TM
\end{lstlisting}

YAML anchors may hold shared sections. Validation E uses one such file for nine
cases and references digitized text data directly; no helper script is needed.

\subsection{Normal and directional emittance}

\begin{lstlisting}
run:
  optics: true
  thermal: false
  plots: false
  write_outputs: true

simulation:
  wavelength: {min: 8.0, max: 13.0, n: 300}
  angles: specific
  polar_angle_deg: 35.0
  azimuth_angle_deg: 90.0
  polarization: TM
  s4_modes: 80
\end{lstlisting}

For normal incidence, set \code{angles: normal}; the requested TE, TM, or both
are still computed explicitly.

\subsection{Hemispherical emittance}

\begin{lstlisting}
simulation:
  wavelength: {min: 3.0, max: 30.0, n: 1000}
  angles: hemispherical
  polarization: unpolarized
  hemisphere_theta_points: 8
  hemisphere_azimuth_points: 12
  s4_modes: 100
\end{lstlisting}

A live S4 thermal case requires \code{angles: hemispherical}: the energy balance
integrates emissivity over the hemisphere, so a single normal-incidence spectrum
does not define it. Convergence must be checked by increasing the polar-angle
count, azimuth count, and S4 Fourier basis size independently.

That requirement is what makes a live thermal run expensive. The solver cost
scales as
\(n_\lambda \times n_\theta \times n_\phi \times n_{\mathrm{pol}}\):
the listing above is \(1000\) wavelengths over \(8\times12=96\) quadrature
directions, plus one zero-weight normal probe, and an unpolarized case evaluates
both TE and TM at every one of those points. Changing a convection coefficient
or an ambient temperature does not change any of it.

The practical workflow is therefore to run the optics once, write the reduced
spectrum, and re-drive the thermal stage from that file with
\code{run.optics\_results}, which costs no S4 time. Validations A, B, and C are
built this way. Validation C keeps the split explicit: four
\code{optics\_*.yaml} cases call S4 and write \code{data/optics/*.txt}, and two
\code{cell\_*.yaml} cases consume those spectra to solve the energy balance.

To chain the two runs without any intermediate step, set
\code{run.optics\_export} in the optics case to a fixed path and point
\code{run.optics\_results} at that same path in the thermal case. These are the
write and read halves of one five-column format
(\code{lambda\_um R T emit abs\_si}). The export is written even when
\code{run.write\_outputs} is false, because it is requested by explicit path.
Note that \code{optics.csv} cannot be reused here: it is comma-separated with a
text header, which the resume reader rejects, and it lands in a timestamped
folder that a later case cannot name in advance. Case A.1 in
\code{validations/} is the minimal two-command example.

A resumed spectrum retains no directional information, so the atmospheric term
is angle-independent whatever the file represents. Be aware that
\code{run.optics\_results\_angles} does not change this, or any other number:
it is recorded in \code{run.json} and in the export header as a statement of
what the stored spectrum is, and \code{normal} and \code{hemispherical} produce
bit-identical results. Driving the thermal stage from a normal-incidence
spectrum remains an angle-independent approximation rather than a hemispherical
calculation, and must be reported as such --- but it is the provenance of the
file, not this key, that makes it so.

\subsection{Thermal case and temperature reduction}

\begin{lstlisting}
thermal:
  ambient_temperature: 300.0
  # Calibrated effective total coefficient; the paper states 6.0.
  convection_coefficient: 12.54
  absorbed_solar_power: 808.0
  reference_temperature: 360.0
  cooling_temperature: {min: 260.0, max: 380.0, n: 121}
  equilibrium: auto
\end{lstlisting}

For a digitized table whose first column is wavelength and later columns are
different measured emittances, set
\code{run.optics\_results\_emittance\_column} to the desired zero-based column.
Column zero is reserved for wavelength.

\section{Outputs and reproducibility}

MATLAB-style output compatibility has been removed. New runs write:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{optics.csv}: selected directional or hemispherical spectrum;
  \item \code{optics\_directional.csv}: every S4 wavelength, polar angle,
        azimuth, polarization, angular weight, \(R,T,A,A_{\mathrm{Si}}\);
  \item \code{iv.csv}, \code{power.csv}, or \code{cooling\_power.csv};
  \item \code{run.json}: full resolved configuration, Git revision, dirty flag,
        interpreter/platform, S4 binary hash, input paths and SHA-256 hashes,
        and scalar results;
  \item figures when \code{run.plots: true}.
\end{itemize}

The \code{thermal\_results} block reports \(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\) and the fill
factor at both the ambient and the equilibrium operating point, so the cost of
running hot can be read off directly. The ambient pair is \code{null} when the
voltage sweep did not reach the ambient \(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\), which is the
highest in the sweep; that is distinct from a genuine zero, and it does not
stop the run, because the equilibrium point is the result.

Set \code{run.write\_outputs: false} for tests or programmatic validation.
Historical MATLAB files remain only as read-only parity fixtures and under the
gitignored \code{archive/}; they are never emitted by the active package.

\subsection{Case detail and reproduction commands}

Every case below reproduces the numbers quoted here on the committed inputs.
The full statement of sources, digitisation provenance, and per-case
limitations lives in \code{validations/README.md}; this section records what
each case establishes and how to re-run it.

\paragraph{A --- Silva-Oelker and Jaramillo-Fernandez, \emph{Opt. Express}
\textbf{30}(18), 32965 (2022), Table 1.}
Five YAML files drive the thermal/PV path from pre-reduced published spectra.
They contain no \code{geometry} block and never call S4, so the paper's
hexagonal hemisphere array is implicit in the supplied spectra. The reduced
inputs carry no angular TE/TM data, so \(P_{\mathrm{atm}}\) uses the
angle-independent approximation. Computed against published, all five rows:
\(J_{\mathrm{sc}}\) within \(3\%\), \(P_{\mathrm{mpp}}\) within \(3\%\),
\(T_{\mathrm{eq}}\) within \(3~\mathrm{K}\), \(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\) within
\(0.02~\mathrm{V}\). Reflected power is the weak quantity, reaching about
\(10\%\).

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
PYTHONPATH=. python "validations/validation A/run_table1_validation.py"
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{A.1 --- hexagonal-cell smoke test.}
Two plain CLI runs chained by \code{run.optics\_export} and
\code{run.optics\_results}. Geometry mirrors Validation A's soda-lime
hemisphere row (\(9~\mathrm{\mu m}\) diameter on \(75~\mathrm{\mu m}\) flat
soda-lime over Si\(_3\)N\(_4\)/Si/Ag) with an \emph{assumed} close-packed
pitch, TE at normal incidence, \code{s4\_modes: 10} and nine slices --- none of
which is converged. It yields \(|R+T+A-1|\le1.1\times10^{-16}\) in memory,
solar-band \(A_{\mathrm{Si}}=0.823\), window emittance \(0.973\),
\(T_{\mathrm{eq}}=317.09~\mathrm{K}\), \(J_{\mathrm{sc}}=365.92~\mathrm{A/m^2}\),
\(V_{\mathrm{oc}}=0.7264~\mathrm{V}\),
\(P_{\mathrm{mpp}}=230.61~\mathrm{W/m^2}\), fill factor \(0.868\), efficiency
\(0.2314\). These land near Table 1 but are not a reproduction of it.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
radcoolpv run "validations/validation A.1/optics_hemisph_sodalime.yaml"
radcoolpv run "validations/validation A.1/pv_hemisph_sodalime.yaml"
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{B --- Le et al., \emph{ACS Photonics} (2026), Figures 4d and 5d.}
Figure 4d runs \code{cooling\_curve} from digitized film spectra at
\(800~\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\) and \(h_c=9~\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}\). Figure 5d
is a digitized curve comparison only: the paper does not supply the raw
commercial-module optics an independent calculation would need.

\paragraph{C --- Zhao et al., \emph{Renewable Energy} \textbf{191} (2022)
662--668.}
The only case exercising the 1-D \code{grating} shape
(\(p=7~\mathrm{\mu m}\), duty \(0.2\), depth \(10~\mathrm{\mu m}\)). The
grating fills silica's \(9~\mathrm{\mu m}\) reststrahlen dip, lifting the
window-averaged emittance from \(0.767\) (planar) to \(0.938\) against the
paper's \(\approx0.90\); the coupled model gives
\(T_{\mathrm{eq}}=337.82~\mathrm{K}\) against \(337.5~\mathrm{K}\). Both
normal-incidence polarizations are evaluated and the 20--80-mode window average
varies by less than \(0.001\). Conditional on a prescribed solar absorptance
\(\alpha=0.95\) and on normal optics used as an angle-independent thermal
input.

\paragraph{E --- Akerboom et al., \emph{ACS Photonics} \textbf{9} (2022)
3831--3840.}
One YAML defines nine named cases. Optics agree in the radiative-cooling band
(\(7.5\)--\(16~\mathrm{\mu m}\) RMSE \(0.025\)--\(0.030\)) but not over the full
\(2\)--\(16~\mathrm{\mu m}\) range for the silica stacks (RMSE \(0.174\)),
because the finite fabricated stack differs from the paper's semi-infinite
silica absorption convention. The thermal half fails on the paper's own terms
and the repository keeps that failure visible rather than tuning it away.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
radcoolpv run "validations/validation E/validation.yaml"
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Standing limitations}

Validation E has not received a fresh Fourier-mode convergence sweep. Its
60-mode patterned result must not be labelled a converged reference solution.

\section{Required verification for new cases}

For every new publication or device case:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item place all geometry, materials, directions, polarization, wavelength,
        and numerical settings in YAML;
  \item record source data and reject unavailable parameters rather than
        inventing them;
  \item verify \(R+T+A\), passivity, material wavelength coverage, and
        layer-resolved absorption;
  \item demonstrate wavelength, Fourier-mode, polar-angle, and azimuth
        convergence as applicable;
  \item distinguish unit tests, MATLAB parity, independent optical validation,
        literature reproduction, digitized comparison, and calibrated fit;
  \item compare published and calculated values with absolute or relative
        errors and retain unresolved failures.
\end{enumerate}
